Part II Reading comprehension (30 points)

Read the passage and choose the best answer to each question.

自动判题
计分

Around the world, farmers are retooling their land to harvest the hottest commodity: sunlight. As the price of renewable energy technology has plummeted and water has gotten scarce, growers are fallowing acreage and installing solar panels. Some are growing crops beneath them, which is great for plants stressed by too many rays. Still others are letting that shaded land go wild, providing habitat for pollinators and fodder for grazing livestock.




According to a new study, this practice of agrisolar has been quite lucrative for farmers in California's Central Valley and for the environment. Researchers looked at producers who had idled land and installed solar, using the electricity to run equipment like water pumps and selling the excess power to utilities. 




The Central Valley is among the most productive agricultural regions in the world, but it's also extremely water-stressed as California whiplashes between years of significant rainfall and drought. To irrigate all those crops, farmers have drawn so much groundwater that aquifers collapse like empty water bottles, making the earth itself sink by many feet.




Farmers can't make their crops less thirsty, so many have been converting some of their acreage to solar. The Central Valley is ideal for this, being mostly flat and very sunny, hence the agricultural productivity. Additionally, farmers have been getting good rates for the electricity that they offset and send back to the grid. 




The study found that by fallowing land for solar panels, food production in the Central Valley dropped by enough calories to feed 86,000 people a year. Yet, by tapping the sun, growers can simultaneously help California reach its goals of deploying renewables and reducing groundwater usage. The tension remains in meeting those objectives while still producing incredible quantities of food.  




But this isn't an either-or proposition: Many farmers are finding ways to grow some crop. The shade reduces evaporation from the soil, allowing growers to water less often. In turn, a wetted landscape cools the panels, improving their efficiency. This is the compromise allowing for both energy independence and food security. 




Farmers are also turning livestock loose to graze under their panels. Their droppings fertilize the soil, leading to more plant growth and more flowers that support native pollinators. The grass is much lusher under the panels, providing grazing services; especially when the sheep have been on site long-term, you can see the added benefits of the manure load.




If a farmer decides not to replace the solar panels at the end of their lifespan, the soil will be refreshed with nutrients and ready to grow more crops. Even if a grower simply lets them sit for decades without any management, the fallowing can restore the soil's health.

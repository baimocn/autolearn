When we think of biodiversity, usually our minds conjure up colorful, lush images of plants, animals, and birds. Maybe you even think of crazy-looking insects. But what most of us rarely consider when we think of biodiversity is the world beneath our feet.




In fact, more creatures live in soil than in any other environment on the earth – worms, archaea, bacteria, fungi, and countless other microbes. A recent study published in Nature Communications has shown that this below-ground biodiversity greatly contributes to the functionality of ecosystems, and is as affected by climate change as much as above-ground biodiversity, though in different ways.




The soil beneath your feet is home to vast and complex webs of life. Soil, rock, and water are their mediums, and many of their interactions have an impact on all life above ground that we're only barely beginning to grasp.




A lot of activity occurs near the surface, involving familiar players like worms, bacteria, fungi, mites, and insects. A good number of them are cleaning and decomposing dead material and recycling nutrients and energy back into the soil to be picked up by plants and have another go around the merry-go-round of life. Some prey on each other, with worms feeding on bacteria or other worms, and mites preying on mites.




And even more do things we don't fully understand – bacteria that consume electricity, tardigrades that survive in space, and the Devil Worm, which lives miles underground in extreme conditions.




For all of our lack of knowledge of the world below, the study points out that one thing is certain: Soil sustains life above ground. Researchers from Peking University and the University of Copenhagen tried to measure the impact of below-ground biodiversity on ecosystem functions. It is already widely known that biodiversity has a large positive impact on the productivity of an ecosystem, but the underground diversity of life is not often taken into consideration when measuring these impacts. The researchers carried out their study on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, which has extensive climate variation, and found that despite the changes in precipitation, temperature, and acidity, the soil biodiversity had a significant impact – 32 percent – on the variation of the ecosystem's functionality as a whole.




Within a gram of soil could lie more species than all of the vertebrates on the earth. Given what we already know about the roles critters underground play in carbon storage and nutrient recycling, coupled with the rising demands for food and the impacts climate change will have on agriculture, it only makes sense to learn as much as we can about the myriad organisms that live in the soil, and how they interact. Thus, we can better predict how environments will be affected by climate change – and how we can adapt.

Read the passage and choose the best answer to each question.

自动判题
计分

With foodborne illnesses causing over nine million infections each year in the U.S., many of which are potentially caused by contaminated produce, farmers and consumers alike are concerned about figuring out how to bring this number down. But one often overlooked method is the fertilizer used to nourish crops and grow food that feeds people across the country and world.




New research from an agricultural experiment at the University of California, Davis, finds that using compost, even animal-based composts that farmers sometimes shy away from, can lead to healthier soils and potentially fewer pathogens when compared to other conventional fertilizers, which are manufactured from minerals and atmospheric gases.




Surprisingly, manure and compost have been viewed as the cause of potential illness, but rarely a cure. Studies have found that the use of raw manure may be related to the presence of foodborne pathogens, and farmers are understandably concerned about this risk. To suppress outbreaks of foodborne disease, farmers have limited the use of compost and manure to prevent pathogens such as salmonella and listeria from contaminating produce.




In this new study led by UC Davis postdoc Naresh Devarajan, researchers leveraged a long-term agricultural experiment to compare bacterial communities between organic and conventional soil management practices. "There is increasing scientific evidence that soil microbes are important to build a healthy soil," says Devarajan. If the compost adds more nutrients for healthy soil microbes, they can flourish and potentially out-compete pathogens for resources.




For the experiment, the scientists applied four different treatments to fields of corn that were various combinations of conventional fertilizer and two other organic practices, in this case, applying composted poultry litter and using cover crops. Cover crops are planted not to harvest but to help with soil erosion and add nutrients to the soil. Poultry litter is a mix of chicken manure, spilled feed, and other poultry-house materials that contain nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. When composted, it reaches a high temperature that can kill pathogens.




The results showed that adding compost and cover crops increased nutrients like phosphorus and nitrate in the soil; this increase changed the bacterial communities. Specifically, harmful bacteria like listeria and salmonella were both suppressed in soils that had compost and cover crops added.




The study suggests that animal-based composts do not promote pathogen survival and may even promote soil bacterial communities that suppress pathogens. However, the underlying mechanism for how exactly the soil microbes can work as a "biocontrol" agent needs to be further studied.




Commercial or synthetic fertilizers are readily available and effective, but they have downsides. "A lot of greenhouse gas production is from the production of nitrogen fertilizer," says a microbial ecologist at Colorado State University. "We should be looking for every opportunity to put our waste to work."
